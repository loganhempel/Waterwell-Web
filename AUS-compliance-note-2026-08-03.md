## 1. Who governs this now

### 1.1 TGA — Therapeutic Goods Administration · **applies directly**
Regulates advertising of **therapeutic goods** (medicines, supplements, herbal products, devices) to Australians, under the Therapeutic Goods Act 1989 and the Therapeutic Goods (Advertising) Code 2021 (still current as of August 2026). The TGA **does not regulate the promotion of health services** — but an ad for a service becomes an ad for therapeutic goods the moment it refers, directly or indirectly, to goods used in the service. TGA guidance: to keep a health-service ad out of scope, "it is best not to refer to any therapeutic goods used in the delivery of the service in the advertisement." Their worked example of a SAFE ad: *"Call our clinic for a consultation to discuss treatment options for migraine"* — a condition, a consultation, no goods.

Sources: [TGA — Advertising a health service](https://www.tga.gov.au/resources/guidance/advertising-health-service) · [TGA — Advertising health services that involve therapeutic goods](https://www.tga.gov.au/resources/guidance/advertising-health-services-involve-therapeutic-goods) · [TGA — Applying the Advertising Code](https://www.tga.gov.au/products/regulations-all-products/advertising/applying-advertising-code)

**Changed from v1:** v1 hedged on whether the TGA could reach a NZ-only advertiser. Delete the hedge. He is an Australian advertiser.

### 1.2 National Code of Conduct for Health Care Workers · **applies directly — and we need to know which state**
This is the regime that actually targets naturopaths. Agreed by COAG in 2015, administered **state by state**. Key advertising clause: a health care worker **must not make claims, in advertising or promotional material, about the efficacy of treatment or services if those claims cannot be substantiated.** Breach can lead to prohibition orders banning practice or advertising to residents of that state, and prohibition orders are **mutually recognised across jurisdictions** — a breach in one state follows him to the others.

Adopted in **NSW, QLD, SA, VIC, WA, ACT, NT and TAS** — effectively nationwide, each with its own administering body (NSW HCCC, Qld Office of the Health Ombudsman, SA/NT HCSCC, Tas Health Complaints Commissioner, etc.).

Sources: [Qld OHO — National Code of Conduct](https://www.oho.qld.gov.au/for-providers/national-code-of-conduct-for-health-care-workers) · [Queensland Health](https://www.health.qld.gov.au/system-governance/policies-standards/national-code-of-conduct) · [Health Complaints Commissioner Tasmania](https://www.healthcomplaints.tas.gov.au/national-code-of-conduct) · [HCSCC NT](https://hcscc.nt.gov.au/codes-of-conduct) · [NHAA — Guide to the Code for Unregistered Practitioners](https://nhaa.org.au/wp-content/uploads/NHAA-2021-Guide-to-the-Code-of-Conduct-for-Unregistered-Healthcare-Practitioenrs_Final_.pdf)

NSW, for example, requires the practitioner to **display a copy of the Code at the premises where they practise** — for a virtual-only practice the website is the practical equivalent, and there is no settled guidance on what satisfies this. **Changed from v1:** v1 said applicability to inbound NZ telehealth was "genuinely unsettled — comply anyway". It is now settled and binding.

### 1.3 Ahpra / National Law s.133 · **still does NOT apply — re-verified 3 Aug 2026**
Naturopathy remains **unregistered**. ARONAH and the Australian Naturopathic Council lodged a formal submission seeking statutory registration under NRAS on 10 March 2026; as of August 2026 **no decision has been made**, and it is actively opposed by ATMS (which represents ~9,500 practitioners). So s.133 does not bind Kohei — **unless he holds an Ahpra registration in some other profession**, in which case Ahpra's rules apply to *all* his advertising including the naturopathy side.

Sources: [ARONAH News](https://www.aronah.org/aronah-news/) · [ATMS response to the ARONAH/ANC submission](https://www.atms.com.au/response-to-the-aronah-anc-submission-seeking-statutory-registration-of-naturopathy-and-western-herbal-medicine-under-nras/) · [ANPA — Professional Regulation](https://anpa.asn.au/professional-regulation/) · [Ahpra — Advertising guidelines](https://www.ahpra.gov.au/Resources/Advertising-hub/Advertising-guidelines-and-other-guidance/Advertising-guidelines.aspx)

**Don't celebrate.** Ahpra's rules remain the de facto benchmark regulators and platforms measure against, and §1.2/§1.4 fill the gap. **Re-check this every ~3 months now** — the trigger is live and closer than it was.

### 1.4 Australian Consumer Law · **applies directly**
ACL s.18 bans misleading or deceptive conduct in trade or commerce; ss.29/33 ban false representations about services. v1 already had this applying via *ACCC v Valve Corp* (overseas business, AU consumers). Now it applies the ordinary way, to an ordinary Australian business.

**This is the clause that kills any "based in NZ" line.** Not an implication problem — a false-statement problem.

Sources: [ACCC — False or misleading claims](https://www.accc.gov.au/business/advertising-and-promotions/false-or-misleading-claims) · [MinterEllison — TGA/ACCC/Ahpra enforcement roundup](https://www.minterellison.com/articles/unlawful-pharma-and-health-service-advertising-in-regulators-sights)

### 1.5 Privacy Act 1988 · **NEW — applies unconditionally**
The Privacy Act's small-business exemption (turnover under $3M) **does not apply to health service providers.** Any organisation providing a health service and holding health information is covered *regardless of turnover*, and **complementary therapists including naturopaths are explicitly named** as covered entities. Health information is "sensitive information" under the Act, which carries a higher consent and handling bar than ordinary personal data.

So: the booking form, intake questionnaire, consult notes, and any functional-test results Kohei holds are all regulated health information, and the Australian Privacy Principles apply in full.

Sources: [OAIC — Rights and responsibilities](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-legislation/the-privacy-act/rights-and-responsibilities) · [OAIC — Health and medical research](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-legislation/the-privacy-act/health-and-and-medical-research)

**Practical effect:** an APP-compliant privacy policy is now a **hard gate**, not the "flag for later, low urgency" item v1 filed it as. It must cover what health information is collected, why, who it is disclosed to, offshore disclosure (relevant — the site, booking tool and any NZ-side infrastructure), how to access/correct it, and how to complain to the OAIC.

**Bottom line:** the operative rules are **TGA Code (if goods are mentioned) + state National Code substantiation rule + ACL + Privacy Act APPs + Google's policies.** Ahpra stays out of frame unless he registers in an Ahpra profession or naturopathy registration lands.

## 2. Google Ads healthcare policy (the gate that blocks unpause)

Unchanged from v1 except where noted.

- **Telemedicine certification risk — now sharper.** Google requires telemedicine providers advertising to Australia to hold LegitScript Healthcare Merchant Certification plus Google certification. v1 noted it was unclear whether a naturopath video consult counts. **The relocation makes this riskier, not safer:** he is now an Australia-based provider delivering health consultations to Australians online, which is a much more natural fit for "telemedicine provider" than a foreign practitioner was. Still unresolved. **Must be settled before unpause.** [Google policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031?hl=en-AU) · [LegitScript telemedicine certification](https://www.legitscript.com/certification/telemedicine/) · [ppc.land — Google AU healthcare policy update](https://ppc.land/google-updates-healthcare-and-medicines-policy-for-australian-advertisers/)

- **Personalised advertising restrictions:** no remarketing/retargeting and no Customer Match for ads about treatment of medical conditions. AU campaigns must be **search-intent only**. [Google — Health in personalized advertising](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/16701855?hl=en)

## 3. ALLOWED in AU ad copy and landing pages

Keep every ad a **service ad, not a goods ad**, with substantiable, non-serious claims.

- Naming the service and modality: *"Naturopathic consultations, online across Australia."*
- **Practitioner credentials, honestly stated — REWRITTEN FROM v1.** State the qualifications actually held and where they were obtained, in the past tense, as education rather than current registration: *"Qualified in New Zealand"* is true and safe. **Do not** write "NZ-registered naturopath" (registration is an association membership, not a statutory registration, and leading with it in Australia now misdescribes his practice), and **do not** write anything implying Australian registration, which does not exist for naturopaths. Simplest compliant option, and the one the shipped copy takes: **say nothing about registration at all.** Nothing requires an affirmative registration statement.
- Conditions addressed, in non-serious form, with soft verbs: *"Support for digestion, energy and sleep"*, *"Book a consultation to discuss your fatigue"* — mirrors TGA's own safe example.
- Process/logistics: consult length, video format, pricing, booking link.
- General wellbeing language: "personalised health plans", "holistic assessment", "work one-on-one with a naturopath".
- Factual, substantiable statements: years in practice, qualifications held, review count where the reviews are real and current.
- Testimonials about **service experience only** (felt listened to, clear explanations) with no clinical outcome and no product mention. Legal for an unregistered practitioner — but safest is none at all in AU-targeted assets.

## 4. BANNED / HIGH-RISK in AU ad copy and landing pages

Carried from v1, with two additions at the end.

- **Naming or hinting at therapeutic goods** in ads or AU landing pages: specific supplements, herbal medicines, "practitioner-only supplements included". Drags the ad into the TGA Code — full compliance burden, permitted-indications limits, testimonial ban. [TGA guidance](https://www.tga.gov.au/resources/guidance/advertising-health-service)
- **Testimonials about therapeutic goods** — banned under Code s.24; practitioner endorsements of products also prohibited (s.24(6)). [TGA — Testimonials and endorsements](https://www.tga.gov.au/products/regulations-all-products/advertising/applying-advertising-code/testimonials-and-endorsements-advertising)
- **Outcome testimonials generally** ("cured my IBS in 3 sessions") — unsubstantiable efficacy claims under the National Code, misleading-conduct bait under ACL s.18.
- **Cure/treat/prevent claims for serious conditions** (restricted representations): diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, depression, heart disease. [TGA — Restricted and prohibited representations](https://www.tga.gov.au/products/regulations-all-products/advertising/applying-advertising-code/restricted-and-prohibited-representations-advertising)
- **Prohibited representations — never:** cancer, STIs, HIV/AIDS, hepatitis C, mental illness. Zero references in any AU-facing asset.
- **Before/after implications** — transformation arcs, "from chronic fatigue to full energy in 8 weeks", case-study narratives. **🔄 AMENDED 14 Aug 2026 — before/after *imagery* is no longer banned outright, see §Amendment below.**
- **"Detox" claims** — "detox programs", "cleanse your liver", "flush toxins". Unsubstantiable, usually implies goods, and pattern-matches Google's unproven-claims filters.
- **Anti-medicine positioning** — "natural alternative to medication", drug names, "get off your meds".
- **Guarantees and speed claims** — "results guaranteed", "feel better in 2 weeks", "works where doctors failed".
- **"Scientifically proven"** without the evidence file already in hand.
- **Implying Australian registration or Ahpra status** — he holds none; National Code and ACL both prohibit it.
- **🆕 Stating or implying the service is delivered from New Zealand** — now factually false. ACL s.18/s.29. This includes "online from NZ", "NZ clinic", "based in New Zealand", "telehealth from New Zealand", and NZ city/geography framing in AU-targeted assets. *(Already stripped from all AU ad copy — verify it stays stripped on the landing page.)*
- **🆕 Referring to functional test kits being posted to patients** in AU-facing assets. A posted test kit is goods used in the service; naming it pulls a service ad into TGA scope. *(Kept in NZ copy, where it is a genuine differentiator and TGA does not apply.)*

## 6. 🆕 The NZ campaigns are now the cross-border case

Direction of travel has reversed. Kohei is in Australia advertising to New Zealand consumers.

- **NZ has no statutory registration for naturopaths either** — "registered" in a NZ context means professional-association membership. Unchanged by the move, but worth stating since it is why *"Registered Naturopath NZ"* was removed from the NZ copy on 3 Aug: it is an unverified claim we have no evidence file for.
- **NZ Fair Trading Act 1986** bans misleading conduct and false representations about services — the NZ analogue of ACL s.18. It applies to conduct directed at NZ consumers regardless of where the trader sits. **The same falsity rule applies in reverse: NZ ads must not imply Kohei is physically in New Zealand.**
- **What stays fine in NZ copy:** "NZ-wide", "consults across New Zealand", "wherever you are in NZ". These describe **who he serves**, which is true. They do not assert where he is.
- **What was removed on 3 Aug:** *"Naturopath Auckland To Dunedin"* (implies a NZ physical footprint) and *"Registered Naturopath NZ"* (unverified registration claim). Same fault class Kohei flagged on the AU side with *"to perth?"*.

## Amendment — 14 August 2026: before/after imagery on the AU page

**Change:** `#results` (the drag-to-compare slider and the three before/after
cards) is now published on `au.html`. It was previously wrapped in `AU:cut`.
`#testimonials` remains cut.

**Who decided:** Kohei. His 6 July "Website update notes" say — *"seems like
before and after photos can be posted as long as staging is consistent and clear
clinical treatment is disclosed. So let's keep the selected Before and After
photos."* Relayed again by Logan on 14 August. It is his practice and his
exposure, and he has evidently researched it.

**Why v2's blanket ban was over-cautious.** v2 listed before/after imagery under
"never". That was a conservative posture, not a statutory prohibition:

- **TGA** governs advertising of therapeutic *goods*. The AU page names no goods
  (the gate enforces this), so the Advertising Code — including its before/after
  rules at s.30 — is not engaged by these photographs.
- **AHPRA's** testimonial and before/after restrictions bind *registered*
  practitioners. Naturopathy is unregistered; he is not an AHPRA practitioner.
- What **does** bind is the **National Code of Conduct** ("must not make claims
  about the efficacy of treatment that cannot be substantiated") and **ACL
  s.18/s.29** (misleading conduct). A before/after photograph *is* an efficacy
  claim, so it must be genuine, representative, and not imply a typical result.

**The four conditions this is permitted under** — two are Kohei's, two follow
from the National Code and ACL:

1. Consistent staging — each pair shown in the same framing.
2. The clinical treatment behind the result is disclosed.
3. Patient permission, with identifying features masked.
4. An explicit "individual results will vary" statement in the same block.

All four are carried by the expanded `p.disclaimer` in `#results`.

**Still not permitted on AU:** outcome testimonials, quantified results
("99.9% clear"), timeframe claims, and any before/after presented alongside a
named product.

**⚠ Unverified by me:** that the photographs are genuinely his patients, that
permission is on file, and that the "over months rather than a single
appointment" characterisation is accurate for every case shown. The disclaimer
asserts all three. Kohei should confirm before launch — the substantiation
